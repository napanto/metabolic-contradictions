# Comparing two findings

Check one extracted finding from each abstract, then compare the findings. Use the abstracts shown here: you do not need full texts or other studies. You are not deciding which study is right. The extracted finding does not summarise the paper: it is chosen because it concerns the same exposure and outcome as the other abstract, and it may be a secondary finding. Check that it is extracted correctly, not that it is the main one.

**General rule.** In every row of the extraction, choose “No” if a field is wrong, imprecise or incomplete, or if it shows “Not extracted” or “Not reported” but the abstract gives that information. Choose “Yes” only if every field in the row matches the abstract.

Read the highlighted sentences in context. The extraction may be wrong; the source takes priority. Publication types from PubMed may help you check the study design, but do not establish the direction of a finding. “Clinical Trial”, for example, does not by itself establish randomisation. The system's prediction for the pair and other readers' answers are hidden.

Answer on your own, without AI tools or help from other people. Before saving you can press “Back”, or “Change” in the summary, and change an earlier choice; later answers will reset when they no longer apply.

## 1. Check the extraction, first A and then B

For each abstract, choose “Yes”, “No” or “Cannot tell” in all seven rows. There are no suggested answers.

You check the finding first, because almost every field refers to that finding, not to the whole study; it may be a secondary one. Right after it you check the comparison and the statistical evidence, which are usually in the same sentence as the finding. The following rows follow the order in which you usually meet the information in the methods of an abstract: aims and design, people, sample size and context. “Study population” and “Comparison group” always appear, with the same entries: the name the extraction gives a group is data to check, not a label. If the extraction says there is no comparison group, that is written in its place, and it can also be an error.

| Check | What to check |
|---|---|
| Finding | What was studied (exposure or intervention), what was measured (the **outcome**, for example LDL cholesterol) and what was found for that outcome (the **result**, for example “lower with psyllium than with placebo”). |
| Comparison | Between groups, a change from baseline, or an association. |
| Statistical evidence | The p-value, interval or wording must support the extracted finding. |
| Study and analysis | Include adjustment and separate subgroup analyses. |
| People and groups | Include the subgroup to which this finding applies. |
| Sample size | Both the number and the group it refers to. |
| Context | Dose, duration, place and biological material. |

**A field marked “Not extracted” or “Not reported” is correct only when the abstract does not give that information; if the text gives it, choose “No”.** A field that does not concern this finding, for example the dose in a study of a biomarker, is correct if it is empty; it is wrong if it contains a value the abstract does not give. “Cannot tell” means that you cannot decide from the text; it is not another way to say “Not reported”.

If you choose “No”, the question “What is wrong? Select all that apply.” appears below: choose every relevant problem among “A value or wording differs from the abstract”, “Not supported by the abstract”, “A value or exception given in the abstract is missing” and “Wrong group or time point”. In the “Finding” row you also find “Wrong direction”, “Wrong exposure or outcome” and “A planned result is presented as observed”.

If you chose “No” or “Cannot tell” in at least one row, after the rows of B the question “Does the extraction error change the finding or the comparison?” appears:

- **“Yes, it changes the finding or the comparison”:** an identified error changes the finding or the comparison. This is an extraction error; no context factor is needed.
- **“No, the finding and the comparison stay the same”:** the error is recorded, then you continue. An omitted sample size, for example, does not always invalidate the finding.
- **“I cannot establish what is being compared”:** there is not enough information for comparison. It appears only when the problem concerns the “Finding” or “Comparison” row. An identified material error takes priority over uncertainty about the other finding.

You are checking the selected finding. A different result omitted from the extraction can be mentioned in the optional note; you do not need to list every result in both abstracts. An omitted exception that changes the scope of the displayed finding is a problem to record.

## Reading the extracted information

The sentences describe the comparison that was extracted. A lower value in one treatment group than another is not the same as a decrease from baseline. A before-and-after comparison does not imply a separate control group. An association does not establish a causal effect.

Groups and their counts are shown separately. Check what each count refers to: enrolled or analysed participants, studies, events, or another unit. Do not calculate an unreported subgroup count by subtraction or split a total in half. Groups may overlap, and different analyses may have different denominators.

A variable used for statistical adjustment is not a subgroup. For example, adjusting for age differs from analysing younger and older people separately. “Biological material” means material such as serum or plasma, not the number of participants.

Statistical details are shown as extracted: p-values, intervals and the authors' wording. Check what each number tests. A p-value for an interaction or change over time is not automatically evidence for the main comparison. A missing p-value does not mean that a result is nonsignificant. A nonsignificant result does not prove no effect, and a wide interval does not establish “no change”. Preserve cautious or tentative conclusions.

**“Not extracted”** means the extraction supplies no detail; check the abstract. **“Not reported”** is an explicit state in the extraction that still needs checking against the source. Neither display is a separate judgment made by the site.

## 2. Decide whether the findings can be compared

Answer these three questions:

1. “Do they concern the same exposure or intervention?”
2. “Do they measure the same outcome?”
3. “Are the measurements and comparisons similar enough?”

Check the reference group, measurement and time point. Different populations or methods may still be comparable. Convertible units do not by themselves prevent comparison. A specific medicine and its whole class are comparable only when the comparison makes sense and that distinction stays visible. If one study finds the effect and the other does not, the findings remain comparable.

If you choose “No” at the third question, at the question “What prevents comparison?” that appears below choose among “Comparison group”, “Type of comparison or time of measurement” (with a short description) and “How the outcome was defined or measured”. Other differences, such as population, design, place, dose, duration or sample size, do not prevent comparison: if the findings are opposite, you will report them in the last question.

A “No” or “Cannot tell” means that the pair cannot be compared, with the reason recorded. If there is more than one reason, at the question “Which of these is the main reason you cannot compare them?” choose the one that determines your decision. Different outcomes, such as HbA1c and heart-failure admissions, do not become contradictory because their results point in different directions.

## 3. Do both studies report the same finding?

Before answering, reread the conclusion of each abstract, not just the direction shown by the extraction: expressions such as “not significant”, “no better”, “did not” or “no difference” indicate an effect that was not confirmed. Choose “Yes” when both report the same direction, or both explicitly report no difference. Different effect sizes can still agree. A difference in statistical significance alone is not an opposite finding.

Choose “No” to describe the difference in the next question, “How do the results differ?”. An explicit conclusion that does not confirm an effect goes there even when the point estimate has the same direction. Two estimates in the same direction do not automatically mean non-confirmation just because one crosses a statistical threshold.

If the abstracts do not let you decide, choose “Cannot tell”.

## 4. How do the results differ?

Choose one:

- “One reports an effect or association; the other reports no effect or does not confirm it”
- “Both find an effect or association, but in opposite directions”
- “They differ only in size, not in direction or conclusion”
- “The abstracts do not give enough information to decide”

An effect versus explicit absence or non-confirmation takes priority over a contextual explanation. This describes what the abstracts report; it does not prove that a formal replication failed. A difference only in size is agreement. Choose “Both find an effect or association, but in opposite directions” only if both studies find an effect: this answer leads to the last question.

## 5. Do the abstracts describe a difference that could explain the opposite findings?

Compare what each abstract says about its own study. The authors do not need to discuss the other study. Choose a difference supported by the texts, not a possible explanation from outside knowledge.

If you choose “Yes”, at the question “Which difference could explain this?” that appears below choose the **best-supported, relevant difference**. You can mention others in the optional note:

- “People studied: age, sex or other conditions”
- “Where participants were recruited”
- “Disease subtype”
- “Study design”
- “Comparison group”
- “A specific medicine versus a medicine class”
- “Dose”
- “Duration or follow-up”
- “How the outcome was defined or measured”
- “Biological material, such as serum or plasma”
- “Statistical analysis or adjustment”
- “Sample size, when it helps explain the finding”
- “Another difference”, with a short description

The difference need only offer a plausible explanation; you do not need to prove its causal role. A difference in sample size alone is not enough without a relevant reason. Different author affiliations do not establish different recruitment regions. A meta-analysis may include the study being compared; mention that dependence instead of treating it as an independent replication.

**“No”** means that the findings are sufficiently comparable and you find no documented difference that could explain the opposite results. It does not claim that the contexts are identical. If essential context is missing, choose “Cannot tell”; if you have already chosen “Yes”, at the question “Which difference could explain this?” choose “Not stated: essential context is missing” or “Cannot determine from these abstracts”. These mean that the comparison cannot be established, not a direct contradiction.

## How the answers are recorded

The path determines one of six categories: extraction error, not comparable, concordant, not replicated, apparent contradiction or direct contradiction. Problems in individual fields are kept separately. There is no context factor for extraction error or direct contradiction. Sample size and statistical analysis are separate details; sample size is never classified as biological material.

You see your own choices, not a suggested category or the system's verdict. When a choice does not convince you, or you pick an unusual option, write a few words in the optional note: they are very useful for the analysis.
