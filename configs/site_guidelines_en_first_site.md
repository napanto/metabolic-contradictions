# Comparing two findings

Check one extracted finding from each abstract, then compare the findings. Use the abstracts shown here: you do not need full texts or other studies. You are not deciding which study is right. The extracted finding need not summarise every result in the paper.

Read the highlighted sentences in context. The extraction may be wrong; the source takes priority. Publication types from PubMed may help you check the study design, but do not establish the direction of a finding. “Clinical Trial”, for example, does not by itself establish randomisation. The system's prediction for the pair and other readers' answers are hidden.

Answer on your own, without AI tools or help from other people. You can go back and change an earlier choice before saving; later answers will reset when they no longer apply.

## 1. Check the extraction, first A and then B

For each abstract, answer **Yes**, **No** or **Cannot tell** on all seven rows. There are no suggested answers.

| Check | What to check |
|---|---|
| Result | What was studied, what was measured, and what was found. |
| People or groups | Include the subgroup to which this finding applies. |
| Sample size | Both the number and the group it refers to. |
| Comparison | Between groups, a change from baseline, or an association. |
| Study and analysis | Include adjustment and separate subgroup analyses. |
| Statistical evidence | The p-value, interval or wording must support the summary. |
| Other details shown | Dose, duration, setting and biological sample. |

**Missing is correct when the abstract does not give the detail. If the abstract gives it but the extraction leaves it out, mark No.** Yes also covers a detail that does not apply and has not been invented. Cannot tell means that you cannot decide from the text; it is not another way to say “not reported”.

On No, select every relevant problem in that row: wrong value or wording; not supported by the abstract; missing detail or exception; wrong group or time point. For the result you can also identify a wrong direction, wrong exposure or outcome, or a planned result presented as observed.

If any row has No or Cannot tell, the next question asks whether the problem changes the finding or comparison:

- **Yes:** an identified error changes the comparison. This is an extraction error; no context factor is needed.
- **No, only a supporting detail:** record the defect, then continue. An omitted sample size, for example, does not always invalidate the finding.
- **I cannot establish what is being compared:** there is not enough information for comparison. An identified material error takes priority over uncertainty about the other finding.

You are checking the selected finding. A different result omitted from the extraction can be mentioned in the optional note; you do not need to list every result in both abstracts. An omitted exception that changes the scope of the displayed finding is a problem to record.

## Reading the extracted information

The sentences describe the comparison that was extracted. A lower value in one treatment group than another is not the same as a decrease from baseline. A before-and-after comparison does not imply a separate control group. An association does not establish a causal effect.

Groups and their counts are shown separately. Check what each count refers to: enrolled or analysed participants, studies, events, or another unit. Do not calculate an unreported subgroup count by subtraction or split a total in half. Groups may overlap, and different analyses may have different denominators.

A variable used for statistical adjustment is not a subgroup. For example, adjusting for age differs from analysing younger and older people separately. “Biological sample” means material such as serum or plasma, not the number of participants.

Statistical details are shown as extracted: p-values, intervals and the authors' wording. Check what each number tests. A p-value for an interaction or change over time is not automatically evidence for the main comparison. A missing p-value does not mean that a result is nonsignificant. A nonsignificant result does not prove no effect, and a wide interval does not establish “no change”. Preserve cautious or tentative conclusions.

**Not extracted** means the extraction supplies no detail; check the abstract. **Not reported** is an explicit state in the extraction that still needs checking against the source. Neither display is a separate judgment made by the site.

## 2. Decide whether the findings can be compared

Answer these three questions:

1. Do the findings concern the same exposure or intervention?
2. Do they measure the same outcome?
3. Are the measurements and comparisons similar enough?

Check the reference group, measurement and time point. Different populations or methods may still be comparable. Convertible units do not by themselves prevent comparison. A specific medicine and its whole class are comparable only when the comparison makes sense and that distinction stays visible.

A No or Cannot tell means that the pair cannot be compared, with the reason recorded. If there is more than one reason, record them and select the one that determines your decision. Different outcomes, such as HbA1c and heart-failure admissions, do not become contradictory because their results point in different directions.

## 3. Do both studies report the same finding?

Choose Yes when both report the same direction, or both explicitly report no difference. Different effect sizes can still agree. A difference in statistical significance alone is not an opposite finding.

Choose No to describe the difference in the next question. An explicit conclusion that does not confirm an effect goes there even when the point estimate has the same direction. Two estimates in the same direction do not automatically mean non-confirmation just because one crosses a statistical threshold.

If the abstracts do not let you decide, choose Cannot tell.

## 4. How do the results differ?

Choose one:

- One reports an effect or association; the other reports no effect or does not confirm it.
- They report effects or associations in opposite directions.
- They differ only in size, not in direction or conclusion.
- The abstracts do not give enough information to decide.

An effect versus explicit absence or non-confirmation takes priority over a contextual explanation. This describes what the abstracts report; it does not prove that a formal replication failed. A difference only in size is agreement. Opposite directions lead to the last question.

## 5. Is there a documented difference that could explain opposite findings?

Compare what each abstract says about its own study. The authors do not need to discuss the other study. Choose a difference supported by the texts, not a possible explanation from outside knowledge.

On Yes, select the **best-supported, relevant difference**. You can mention others in the optional note:

- People studied: age, sex or other conditions.
- Where participants were recruited.
- Disease subtype.
- Study design.
- Comparison group.
- A specific medicine versus a medicine class.
- Dose.
- Duration or follow-up.
- How the outcome was defined or measured.
- Biological material, such as serum or plasma.
- Statistical analysis or adjustment.
- Sample size, when relevant to the explanation.
- Another difference, with a short description.

The difference need only offer a plausible explanation; you do not need to prove its causal role. A difference in sample size alone is not enough without a relevant reason. Different author affiliations do not establish different recruitment regions. A meta-analysis may include the study being compared; mention that dependence instead of treating it as an independent replication.

**No** means that the findings are sufficiently comparable and you find no documented difference that could explain the opposite results. It does not claim that the contexts are identical. If essential context is missing, choose Cannot tell, “Not stated: essential context is missing”, or “Cannot determine from these abstracts”. These mean that the comparison cannot be established, not a direct contradiction.

## How the answers are recorded

The path determines one of six categories: extraction error, not comparable, concordant, not replicated, apparent contradiction or direct contradiction. Problems in individual fields are kept separately. There is no context factor for extraction error or direct contradiction. Sample size and statistical analysis are separate details; sample size is never classified as biological material.

You see your own choices, not a suggested category or the system's verdict. The optional note can explain a detail that matters to you.
